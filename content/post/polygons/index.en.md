Parable of the Polygons is an interactive article about how small individual preferences can create large-scale social segregation. By moving triangle and square residents, you can observe how segregation can emerge even when individuals do not hold strong biases.

## What you'll learn

- How a tiny individual preference, multiplied across a population, produces large-scale collective segregation
- How a "discriminatory outcome" can emerge without any individual holding "discriminatory intent"
- Why segregation, once formed, doesn't undo itself just by removing bias — it takes an active demand for diversity

## Walkthrough

This explorable is built as a sequence of stages. Work through them from top to bottom.

1. **Move residents by hand**: Each resident follows one rule — "I wanna move if less than 1/3 of my neighbors are like me." Drag unhappy shapes (the ones highlighted) into empty spots until nobody is unhappy anymore. Watch how the neighborhood splits into triangle-only and square-only clusters, even though no one intended that.
2. **Run the automatic simulation**: The same rule now runs automatically, with a line graph tracking segregation over time. Re-run it a few times and see whether the outcome is consistent.
3. **Adjust the bias slider**: This slider controls the threshold — what share of neighbors must match for a shape to be happy. Compare what happens as you push the threshold above 33%, and again at 50%.
4. **Lower the bias in an already-segregated world**: Start from a segregated state and bring the threshold down toward zero. Notice that an existing segregation pattern does not undo itself just because bias disappears.
5. **Flip the bias toward diversity**: Instead of "I'm fine with up to 90% of the same shape," give residents the opposite preference — a small desire for some variety. Watch a segregated world spontaneously mix back together.
6. **Compare at scale**: Vary both the bias and the anti-bias strength on a larger population and observe the effect.
7. **Box of Friendship and beyond**: The closing minigame and sandbox let you combine everything you've tried so far.

## Background

This simulation builds on the 1971 paper "Dynamic Models of Segregation" by Nobel laureate game theorist Thomas Schelling. Schelling showed, with a mathematical model, that even when individuals hold only a mild preference for neighborhood homogeneity, the collective outcome can be strong segregation.

Parable of the Polygons takes the model a step further, showing that a small preference for diversity can undo segregation just as a small bias can create it. For empirical validation against real-world data, see W.A.V. Clark's 1991 paper "A Test of the Schelling Segregation Model."

## Takeaways

- A small individual bias compounds into a large collective bias
- Segregation, once formed, doesn't dissolve on its own just by removing bias
- Actively demanding a bit more diversity around you is a practical way to reverse segregation
